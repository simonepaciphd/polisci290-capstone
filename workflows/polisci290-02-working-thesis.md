# Workflow 02 — The working thesis

**When:** week 2, before Sun, Oct 4. **Class:** Session 2, drawing insights from the scientific literature. **Feeds:** the **Working Thesis** milestone, at least 350 words and 5 citations, and later the argument section of your paper.

## Goal

Move from a question to an **answer**: a thesis you can defend, the **mechanism** that makes it work, and the **literature** it builds on. It's a *working* thesis: it can change as your evidence comes in.

The syllabus asks for three things: propose an answer, strategy, or solution; draw insights from political science literature; describe the mechanisms behind the thesis.

## You produce

A Working Thesis milestone (at least 350 words, at least 5 citations) with:

1. **Thesis**: 1–3 sentences. A claim, not a topic: *what* you argue.
2. **Mechanism**: the step-by-step process that connects your cause to your outcome, or your premises to your recommendation: *how* it works. Who are the actors, what do they want, and which institutions constrain them?
3. **Literature**: at least 5 sources, each doing a job for your argument:
   - **allies**, which support a step of your mechanism or show how others measured your variables;
   - at least one **foil**, a competing argument you take on;
   - ideally one **template**, a study that does something like what you want to do.
4. **Scope**: where and when your thesis applies.

## Steps

1. **You.** Write a first version of your thesis, in one sentence. State the direction: does X increase or decrease Y? If your project recommends something, state the recommendation and its main reason.
2. **You.** Build the argument yourself first. Pick an approach from Session 2:
   - *inductive*: start from a concrete case of your topic, and ask what explains it;
   - *deductive*: start from a general model (actors, incentives, institutions) and ask what it predicts in your case.

   Write the chain: X happens, which leads A to do B, which produces Y. Aim for 3–5 links.
3. **With AI (optional).** Brainstorm, then challenge, the argument, as in the Session 2 workshop. Paste in your question, your thesis, and your chain:

   ```
   You are a political science mentor helping me develop a working thesis for my capstone.
   Here is my question, my one-sentence thesis, and the step-by-step mechanism I have in mind.
   Do not write the thesis for me.
   1. Tell me whether my thesis is a claim or a topic, and whether a reasonable person could disagree with it.
   2. Walk through my mechanism one link at a time. Point out any missing step between cause and effect,
      and tell me which link is weakest.
   3. Brainstorm 3 other mechanisms that could connect the same cause and outcome, with the actors involved.
   4. Suggest the strongest competing argument (a foil), stated the way its supporters would put it.
   5. Ask me where and when my argument should apply, and when it might break down.
   ```

4. **You decide.** Settle your thesis and mechanism. Keep what you can defend. Choose the foil you'll take on.
5. **You.** Find your literature. Start with Google Scholar: search by concept (for example, "descriptive representation" AND "turnout"), not by topic. When you find a strong paper, use its bibliography and its "Cited by" list. AI search tools (such as Elicit, or a "deep research" report) can help you find leads.
6. **With AI (optional).** Map the sources onto your argument. Paste in your thesis, mechanism, and the list of sources with one line on each:

   ```
   Here is my thesis, my mechanism, and the sources I found, with one line on what each says.
   1. For each source, tell me what job it could do: support a step of my mechanism, show how to measure a
      variable, serve as a foil, or model the analysis I want to do.
   2. Tell me which steps of my mechanism have no support yet.
   3. Tell me whether I have a real foil, or only sources that agree with me.
   Do not add new sources to my list. If you mention any study, remind me that I have to verify it exists.
   ```

7. **You.** Verify every source: it exists, you've read at least the abstract and the relevant section, and it says what you'll use it for. **AI tools invent citations.**
8. **You.** Write the milestone in your own words. Argue, don't list: "Smith (2020) found X" is a summary; "Because incumbents respond to local media (Smith 2020), a newspaper closure should…" is an argument.

## Check before moving on

- [ ] Your thesis is a claim a reasonable person could disagree with.
- [ ] Your mechanism has no black boxes: every link is a concrete claim about actors or institutions.
- [ ] You know which link is weakest.
- [ ] At least 5 citations, each doing a job, including at least one foil.
- [ ] Every source is verified.
- [ ] At least 350 words, in your own words, with an AI-use note attached.

## Common traps

- **A topic sentence instead of a thesis.** "This paper examines X and Y" works just as well for a paper that reaches the opposite conclusion.
- **A book-report literature review.** Every source should do a job for *your* argument.
- **All allies, no foil.** If nobody disagrees with you, either the thesis is too safe or you haven't found the critics.
- **Unverified citations.** A citation you can't find is a citation you can't use.
- **Freezing the thesis.** It's a working thesis. If the data plan or the results push back, you'll revise it in week 6.
