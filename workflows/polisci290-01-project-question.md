# Workflow 01 — Revisiting your project question

**When:** week 2, early in the week. You already submitted the Project Question (Sun, Sep 27). **Feeds:** the Working Thesis (Oct 4), and later your introduction.

## Goal

You've written a question. Before you build a thesis on it, take one more pass to make sure it's **specific, answerable, and motivated**. A sharper question now saves you weeks later. This week's work is optional, but most questions get better with a second look.

## You produce

- **Your question, revised or confirmed**: 1–2 sentences, ending in a question mark.
- **Its type**: *positive* (what is happening, and why?), *normative* (what should happen?), or *interpretive* (what does it mean to the people involved?). See Session 1A.
- **Your key variables**: the cause and the outcome (or the main concepts), each with a one-line definition.
- **One line on what changed**, and why. If nothing changed, say why the question holds.

## Steps

1. **You.** Reread your submitted Project Question, and any feedback you got in class or from the instructor. Write down, in one sentence, what you actually want to know.
2. **You.** Check it against Session 1B. Name your key variables. For each, write what it means in your project (the *systematized concept*) and one way you could observe it.
3. **With AI (optional).** Stress-test the question. Paste in your Project Question milestone and your notes from steps 1–2:

   ```
   You are a research-design mentor for a political science capstone seminar.
   Here is the project question I submitted and my notes on its key variables. Do not rewrite it for me.
   1. Tell me whether my question is a topic in disguise, and why.
   2. Ask me who, where, when, and what outcome, one at a time, until the question is specific.
   3. Tell me whether the question is positive, normative, or interpretive, and whether it assumes its own answer.
   4. Check that my key variables are distinct concepts I could observe, and flag any that are too vague.
   5. Ask me "so what?" twice: who should care about the answer, and what would they do with it?
   6. Suggest 2 sharper versions that go in different directions, and say what evidence each would need.
   ```

4. **You decide.** Keep your question, revise it, or combine it with one of the versions. The question has to be one you understand and care about. Check two things: could an answer you *dislike* come out of it, and is it small enough for ten weeks?
5. **You.** Write down the final question, its type, your key variables, and one line on what changed. Carry them into Workflow 02.

If the AI suggested a framing you adopted, note it in your AI-use note for the Working Thesis.

## Check before moving on

- [ ] A stranger could read your question and know exactly what you're investigating.
- [ ] You can name the question type, and your key variables.
- [ ] Each key variable has a definition and at least one way to observe it.
- [ ] You can say who would care about the answer, and why.
- [ ] The final wording is yours.

## Common traps

- **Topic, not question.** If it has no question mark, it isn't a question.
- **Smuggling in the answer.** "Why does social media destroy democracy?" assumes that it does.
- **Changing the question every week.** Revise it now if you need to. After the Data Plan, change it only if the evidence forces you to.
- **Taking the AI's version as is.** Its suggestions are options, not your question.
