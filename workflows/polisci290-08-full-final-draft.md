# Workflow 08 — The full final draft

**When:** week 8, before Sun, Nov 15. **Class:** Session 8, formulating conclusions and recommendations. **Feeds:** the **Full final draft** milestone, at least 20 pages.

## Goal

Complete the paper, and get its **framing** right: an introduction that promises exactly what the paper delivers, and a conclusion that says what your findings mean, for whom, and what to do next. The syllabus asks for two things: focus on the introduction and conclusion; finalize the framing of the project.

## You produce

A complete draft of at least 20 pages with:

1. **An introduction** that states the problem, your answer, your evidence in a sentence, and why it matters to your audience, all within the first page.
2. **A conclusion** that restates what you found (not what you hoped to find), states the implications, and names what should come next.
3. **Recommendations**, if your format calls for them (strategy memo, green paper, white paper, risk report), each tied to a specific finding.
4. **An executive summary or abstract** of about 150–250 words (suggested; it helps you, and it becomes the core of your final presentation).




## Steps

1. **You.** Write the executive summary first, as in the Session 8 workshop: problem, answer, evidence, implications, in about 200 words. If you can't write it, the framing isn't settled yet.
2. **You.** Reread your introduction next to your findings. Rewrite the introduction to match what you actually found. The version you drafted in week 5 almost certainly promises something slightly different.
3. **With AI (optional).** Check the arc of the paper. Paste in the introduction, the conclusion, and your executive summary:

   ```
   You are an editor for an applied political science capstone written for [audience].
   Here are my introduction, my conclusion, and my executive summary. Do not rewrite them.
   1. Tell me whether the introduction promises more, less, or something different from what the conclusion delivers.
   2. Tell me whether my answer appears on the first page, and whether a busy reader would find it.
   3. For each recommendation or implication, tell me which finding supports it, and flag any that go beyond my evidence.
   4. Ask me what the next study should be, and whether my conclusion says so.
   ```

4. **You decide.** Settle the framing: the one claim the paper stands on, and the audience it's for. Decide which recommendations your evidence can carry.
5. **You.** Write the conclusion in your own words. Turn results into recommendations only as far as the evidence goes: a correlational finding supports "consider" or "pilot", not "adopt everywhere". Think about scale: would what worked in your cases work at scale (the Session 8 podcast)?
6. **You.** Fill the remaining placeholders, so every section is complete.
7. **With AI (optional).** Review the full draft for integration:

   ```
   Here is my full draft. Do not rewrite it.
   1. Tell me where the paper reads like separate milestones stapled together, and where a transition is missing.
   2. Tell me where the same point is made more than twice.
   3. Check that my key terms and variable names are the same throughout.
   ```

## Check before moving on

- [ ] Your answer is on page one.
- [ ] The introduction and conclusion promise and deliver the same thing.
- [ ] Every recommendation is tied to a finding, and calibrated to it.
- [ ] No placeholders are left.
- [ ] At least 20 pages, in your own words, with an AI-use note attached.

## Common traps

- **An introduction written in October.** Rewrite it to match what you found.
- **A conclusion that only summarizes.** Say what it means and what to do next.
- **Recommendations the evidence can't carry.** Match the strength of the recommendation to the strength of the finding.
- **A new argument in the conclusion.** If it's important, it belongs in the body.
