# Workflow 11 — The final research presentation



**When:** the **Final Research Conference**, in class, **Mon, Nov 30 / Wed, Dec 2**. **Feeds:** the final research presentation (10%) and your final paper.

## Goal

Pitch your project to the class: **one clear idea**, the evidence behind it, and why it matters. Then use the audience's questions to improve the final paper.

| | Final research presentation |
|---|---|
| When | Final Research Conference, Nov 30 / Dec 2 |
| Length | **10 minutes**, followed by Q&A |
| Slides | **5–10 slides** |
| What you present | The problem, your argument, your evidence, your key results |
| Graded? | Yes, 10% (rubric in `docs/polisci290-rubrics.md`) |

The syllabus asks for four things: pitch the project (what's the motivating problem, and why is it interesting?); briefly illustrate your argument, thesis, or solution; illustrate your empirical strategy, data sources, and analysis; discuss and visualize your key results.

## You produce

- **One-sentence takeaway**: what the audience should remember. It must be a *claim*, not a topic.
- **A hook**: your first 30 seconds. A surprising fact, a concrete case, or a tension your project resolves.
- **A slide plan** and a timed rehearsal.
- **Q&A prep**: the 3 most likely questions, one hard question, and your short answers.

## A 10-minute structure (suggested)

| Slides | Content | Time |
|---|---|---|
| 1 — The problem | Hook, your question, why it matters | ~1 min |
| 2 — Your answer | Your thesis and mechanism, as a simple diagram if possible | ~1.5 min |
| 3–4 — Your evidence | Design, data sources, and analysis | ~1.5 min |
| 5–7 — What you found | Your key results, visualized, and the main limitation | ~3 min |
| 8 — What it means | Your takeaway, and what your audience should do | ~1 min |

That's about 8 minutes: plan for about 80% of your time, because everything takes longer live. Q&A comes after the 10 minutes. Results get the most time: they're what the audience came for.

## Steps

1. **You.** Start from your executive summary (Workflow 08). What is done, and what is still in progress? Present what's done, and be clear about the rest.
2. **You.** Write a first version of your takeaway sentence. If you can't say it in one sentence, the talk isn't focused yet.
3. **With AI (optional).** Test the takeaway and the outline:

   ```
   You are a demanding audience member at a student research conference in political science.
   Here is my one-sentence takeaway, my main findings, and my slide outline for a 10-minute talk.
   1. Tell me whether my takeaway is a claim or a topic.
   2. Suggest 2 alternative takeaways my results could support, and say which is strongest.
   3. Tell me where the outline spends too long on setup and too little on results.
   4. Suggest one visual per slide that could replace text (a chart, a map, a diagram, a document).
   ```

4. **You decide.** Settle the takeaway and the slide order.
5. **You.** Build the slides, using Session 9: one idea per slide, few words, large type, a real visual where you can. Use the figures you polished in week 9. AI can help you make charts (for example, by writing the plotting code); the talk itself is yours.
6. **You.** Rehearse with a timer, out loud, twice. Don't read your slides.
7. **With AI (optional).** Practice the Q&A:

   ```
   Here is my takeaway and my speaker notes.
   Ask me the three questions a seminar audience is most likely to ask, one at a time, and react to my answers.
   Then ask me the hardest fair question you can think of.
   ```

   Most audiences ask some version of: What about [alternative explanation]? How do you know it isn't [reverse causation / selection / a measurement artifact]? Does this generalize beyond your cases?

## Check before moving on

- [ ] Your takeaway is one sentence, and it's a claim.
- [ ] 5–10 slides, 10 minutes (Q&A comes after), timed in rehearsal.
- [ ] Results get the most time.
- [ ] You have short answers ready for three likely questions and one hard one.
- [ ] You don't present plans as results.
- [ ] After the conference: you've written down the feedback you received, and what you'll change in the final paper.

## Common traps

- **Too much setup, not enough results.**
- **A literature review on a slide.** One sentence of context is usually enough.
- **Walls of text on slides.**
- **A hook that doesn't lead anywhere.** It has to set up your takeaway.
- **Defensive answers.** When a question is hard: acknowledge it, answer what you can, say what you can't, and move on.
