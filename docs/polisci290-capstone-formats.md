

# Capstone formats

## Pick a field and a format

The capstone is meant to **replicate how political science is applied in professional settings**. You choose a field and a format that fits your interests. The main options:

| Field | Potential capstone format |
|---|---|
| Research | Research paper / report |
| Journalism | Long-form article |
| Campaigning / Polling | Strategy memo for a campaign |
| Lobbying | Strategy memo for a campaign |
| Public Policy | Green paper (policy proposal report) |
| Diplomacy | Green paper (policy proposal report) |
| Management consulting | White paper (firm policy report) |
| Nonprofit management | White paper (firm policy report) |
| Political risk | Risk analysis report |

If you have a field or format in mind that isn't listed, talk to the instructor at the beginning of the quarter.

## What every format has in common

Whatever the format, the project must:

- identify a **real-world problem or question**;
- apply **theoretical and analytical insights from political science**;
- use an **empirical methodology** to support its claims with data-driven evidence;
- meet the same milestones, lengths, and formatting rules (see `polisci290-00-project-overview.md`).

The format changes **who you write for, what you lead with, and how the sections are arranged**. It doesn't change how strong the argument and the evidence have to be.

## How the formats differ (suggested guidance)

| Format | Reader | Leads with | Typical structure |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Research paper / report** | Scholars and analysts | The puzzle and the answer | Introduction · argument and literature · research design · findings · robustness and limits · conclusion |
| **Long-form article** | An educated general reader | A story, a case, or a surprising fact | Narrative opening · the question · what the evidence shows · who disagrees, and why · what it means. Sources cited; methods explained in plain language, with detail in a note or appendix |
| **Strategy memo for a campaign** | A campaign manager, or an advocacy or lobbying lead | The recommendation | Summary and recommendation · the situation · the evidence (voters, districts, messages, coalitions) · options and trade-offs · the plan and its risks |
| **Green paper** | Policymakers and their staff | The problem, and the options | Summary · the problem and its scale · what the evidence says about causes · policy options, compared against stated criteria · a recommended option and its trade-offs · implementation |
| **White paper** | A firm's or nonprofit's leadership | The problem for the organization | Summary · the challenge · the political or policy environment, with evidence · options for the organization · recommendation · risks and next steps |
| **Risk analysis report** | Decision-makers exposed to political risk | The risk assessment | Summary of the risk and its likelihood · drivers and mechanisms · indicators and evidence · scenarios · what to watch, and how to respond |

The applied formats (memo, green paper, white paper, risk report) usually move the detailed research design to a shorter section or an appendix. It still has to be there: a reader should be able to check how you know what you claim.

## Choosing

1. **Start from your question**, not from the format. Which reader most needs your answer?
2. **Check your evidence.** A risk report needs indicators you can track; a green paper needs options you can compare; a research paper needs a design that tests a thesis.
3. **Read one real example** of your format before the rough draft (week 5): a real think-tank report, campaign memo, or long-form article. Notice what it leads with and how it uses evidence.
4. **Decide by the rough draft.** You can change format before then; after, it gets expensive.
